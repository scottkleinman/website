Grammar Cheat Sheet

This cheat sheet is designed to allow you to quickly analyse modern English grammar. This is not a complete account of English grammar, but it will go a long ways towards helping you analyse sentences in English. Each section ends with an explanation of how you can describe the form and/or function of a word. This knowledge can be valuable in understanding how it is being used in a sentence.

Parts of Speech

Words are classified into different "parts of speech" depending on how they function in a sentence. The traditional parts of speech in English are given below, along with some formal definitions:

  • Noun: A word that identifies a person, place, thing, or abstract idea.
  • Pronoun: A word that stands in for a noun.
  • Article: A word that helps refer to a noun.
  • Adjective: A word that describes a noun.
  • Adverb: A word that describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb.
  • Preposition: A word that links a noun to another word.
  • Particle: A separate word that forms part of the meaning of a verb.
  • Conjunction: A word that connects clauses, sentences, or words.
  • Verb: A word that identifies an action or a state.

There are a number of other minor parts of speech, but they are uncommon or require discussion beyond the scope of this cheat sheet.

If you cannot identify a word's part of speech from the definitions above, you can generally find it by looking it up the word in a good dictionary.

 Warning!

Some words can change their part of speech, depending on the context:

  • I am reading. (verb)
  • Reading is fun. (noun)

Forms v. Meanings

In the discussion below, we will distinguish between the form of the word and its meaning. We may have a word girls which is distinguished from other forms of the word such as girl or girl's. The meaning of this form is plural, as opposed to singular or possessive. In the rest of this cheat sheet, be alert to the use of these terms.

Declensions

In English, nouns and some pronouns are "declined", which means that they change their form depending on the function in the sentence. A table showing all the forms of a noun or pronoun is called a declension.

Nouns are described in terms of features called case and number. Number can be singular or plural, but case is more complicated. Earlier in history, there were a number of case forms, but today there are only possessive and non-possessive forms, as shown in the declension of the word man:

Case Singular Plural
  man men
Possessive man's men's

For further discussion, see the section on Nouns below.

Pronouns have a fuller set of case forms, which are described as subjective, possessive, and objective forms. The subjective case is used when the pronoun is performing the action indicated by an accompanying verb. The possessive case is used to indicate possession (obviously), and the objective case is used for everything else. We can see this in the sentences below, where the pronoun is in bold:

  • I see him. (subjective case)
  • That's mine. (possessive case)
  • He sees me. (objective case)

A declension of the word I (and, for good measure, its plural form we) looks like this:

Case Singular Plural
Subjective I we
Possessive mine ours
Objective me us

 Note

Some possessive pronouns have alternative forms if they are functioning as adjectives. See the section on Personal Pronouns below.

There are times when it will be useful to be able to identify the case and/or number of a pronoun to figure out how it is functioning in a sentence. When in doubt, you can write out a declension showing all the case forms in order to help you decide.

Conjugations

Verbs also change their forms depending on the function in the sentence. However, a table containing these forms is called a conjugation, and the verb is "conjugated".

 What's the difference between a "declension" and a "conjugation"?

Nouns and pronouns are "declined" (described in declensions) and verbs are "conjugated" (described in conjugations). Technically, a declension lists all forms of a noun or pronoun, whereas a conjugation only lists forms in the present and past tense. See the section on Verbs for an account of the different types of verbs, which will make this distinction clearer.

Verbs are described in terms of features: number and person. As with nouns and pronouns, number can be singular or plural. The person feature identifies who (or what) is doing the action or experiencing the state indicated by the verb. The table below indicates how to describe the person feature:

Description Meaning Examples
First person Refers to the person addressing the audience. I speak, We speak
Second person Refers to the person being addressed. You speak
Third person Refers to anyone else. He speaks, She speaks, It speaks, They speak

 Note

In present-day English, all the forms of verb are same, regardless of person, except in the third person singular present tense. Compare the past tense I speak, He speaks, They speak, but past tense I spoke, He spoke, They spoke.

Some pronouns called personal pronouns also have person as a feature. In the table above, each of the pronouns (I, we, you, etc.) can be described as "first person singular", "first person plural", "second person singular", and so on.

Another feature of verbs is tense, which refers to whether the action or state is experienced in present or the past. When writing out the conjugation of a verb, it is easiest to present and past tense forms in separate tables like the ones below.

Present Tense

First speak speak
Second speak speak
Third speaks speak

Modifiers and Phrases

Nouns and verbs can be accompanied by other words that further describe their meaning. These words are called modifiers. Modifiers may be adjectives, articles, or adverbs. For instance, nouns may be modified by adjectives (e.g. green thumb) or articles (the thumb). Verbs may be modified by adverbs (e.g. go quickly) or particles (to go). Adjectives and adverbs can also be modified by adverbs (a very green thumb, to go very quickly).

A word plus all of its modifiers is called a phrase. Phrases can sometimes consist of longer sequences of words, but you can identify them by checking whether you can replace them with a single noun (for a noun phrase) or a single verb (for a verb phrase).

One important skill is to recognise where the boundaries are between phrases. This allows you to see how they relate to each other. A recognition of phrasal boundaries is also the basis for the use of punctuation (not the other way around).

Clauses

A clause is made up of a special kind of noun phrase called the subject and a special kind of verb phrase called the predicate. A predicate must have a verb with a present or past tense form, and the subject must indicate who or what is doing the action or experiencing the state indicated by the verb phrase of the predicate.

Pronouns in the subject go in the subjective case form. Another way to describe a predicate is that it is a verb phrase with a finite verb. For that terminology, see the section on Verbs below.

A sentence must have at least one clause. If there are multiple clauses, one is the main clause, and the others are dependent clauses (typically introduced by conjunctions). As with phrases, it is important to see where the boundaries are between clauses, especially to understand how punctuation is used.

Nouns

A noun is succinctly described as a person, place, or thing, although this also includes abstract ideas like "happiness". Nouns generally form their plural and possessive forms with -s: one girl, two girls, the girl's book, the girls' books.

 Warning

Although the plural and possessive forms are pronounced the same, in writing they are distinguished by the use of apostrophes by those who are not doomed to eternal damnation!

English has many irregular nouns (man/men, goose/geese, sheep/sheep, etc.), which are typically ancient words or words borrowed from other languages.

Pronouns

Pronouns are words that can function as a noun phrase and typically refer back to some noun phrase called the "antecedent". For instance, in the following sentences, "he" is the pronoun and "the professor" is its antecedent:

The professor spoke. He explained the grammatical form.

There are several different types of pronouns.

Personal Pronouns

Personal pronouns convey whether the noun phrase being referred to is to be understood in the first, second, or third person. The singular forms are I, you, he, she, it, and the plural forms are we and they. In Standard English, there are no separate second person singular and plural forms. In the third person singular, there are separate masculine, feminine, and neuter (not "neutral") forms. Where it is necessary to refer to this feature, it is called gender.

Personal pronouns may also be in the subjective, objective, or objective case. That means that a grammatical description of the personal pronoun I would be "first person singular subjective" For it, the description would be "third person singular neuter" (without context, you cannot tell whether the case is subjective or objective because the form is the same.  Possessive Adjectives

Possessive pronouns can function as adjectives, in which case they are called possessive adjectives. Possessive adjectives often change their form. Compare It is mine (possessive pronoun) to It is my book (possessive adjective). The possessive adjective forms corresponding to the possessive pronouns in the declension above are my, your, his, her, its, our, and their.

Interrogative Pronouns

Interrogative pronouns are used to introduce a question. The primary forms are who, what, and which. The word who has subjective, possessive, and objective case forms (who, whose, whom).

Relative Pronouns

The relative pronouns who, that, and which connect clauses in one part of a sentence to another where the pronoun refers back to a noun phrase in another part of the sentence (the "antecedent"). Here are some examples (the pronoun is in bold and the noun phrase it refers to is in italics):

  • The man who arrived early got a prize.
  • The man whom I met yesterday arrived early got a prize.
  • The book, which I really liked, was long.
  • The book that I read yesterday was long.

You may have a number of questions about these examples. Why is the objective case form whom used in the second example above? Why is word that not used in any of the first three sentences? Standard English has some complex, and not entirely consistent, rules around which relative pronouns. These rules fall outwith the scope of this cheat sheet. However, the sentences above nicely illustrate how the relative pronouns refer back to noun phrases.

All you really need to do to describe these forms is to identify their case and their antecedents.

Articles

English has an "indefinite article" a (an before a vowel) and a "definite article" the. These articles modify nouns and thus form parts of noun phrases.

You only need to be able to describe articles as definite or indefinite.

Adjectives

Apart from articles, most other modifiers of nouns are adjectives (or other parts of speech functioning as adjectives).

 Hint

The most common adjectives formed from other parts of speech are possessive pronouns (e.g. my book) and the past participle form of verbs (e.g. broken record).

Many adjectives have comparative and superlative forms. For instance, the comparative form of big is bigger, and the superlative form is biggest. Adjectives that do not have comparative and superlative forms can be given comparative and superlative meanings with the adverbs more and most: e.g. he is more comfortable, that is the most amazing thing.

Adjective Descriptions

  • plain adjective
  • possessive adjective
  • comparative adjective
  • superlative adjective.

Adverbs

Adverbs modify verbs and typically describe something about manner in which the verb's action takes place. Many adverbs are formed by adding -ly to an adjective (e.g. quickly). But some are repurposed adjectives (e.g. he runs fast or prepositions he looks up).

Many adverbs have comparative and superlative forms ending in -er and -est. Adverbs that don't follow this pattern are preceded by more and most.

There is a group of interrogative adverbs that introduce questions: where, why, how, and these can sometimes be relative adverbs (e.g. I don't know where he lives), which link a clause back to an earlier verb phrase. There are also adverbs called conjunctive adverbs like thus and however.

Adverb Descriptions

  • plain adverb
  • comparative adverb
  • superlative adverb.
  • one of the other types of adverb: interrogative, relative, conjunctive

Prepositions

Prepositions are small function words that introduce noun phrases (e.g. over the moon, to infinity and beyond). The noun phrase is called the object of the preposition and, if it is a pronoun, it goes in the objective case (to me, to whom).

 Ending Sentences with Prepositions

Because a preposition must be followed by an object, by definition, you cannot end a sentence with a preposition. Compare the following sentences:

  • He walked up the road. (preposition)
  • He looked up. (adverb)
  • He looked it up. (particle)

Since prepositions can often function as adverbs or particles, the only challenge is to identify when they are prepositions. Hint: The word to is a preposition when it introduces and object but a particle when it introduces a verb (e.g. to go).

Particles

As noted above the word to is a particle when it introduces a verb, specifically, the infinitive form (see under Verbs below).

English has many compound verbs like to look up (as in a dictionary) where an adverb/preposition follows a verb to create a new meaning. In these compound verbs, the adverb/preposition is considered a particle. Be careful of a sentence like the following:

He looked up the word.

It looks like up is a preposition and the word is its object. But here up is a particle because it forms part of the meaning of the verb (to find a reference), rather than its prepositional meaning which indicates upward direction.

Conjunctions

Conjunctions are small words that connect phrases and clauses. The most common are the coordinating conjunctions: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, and so. (You may have learnt the acronym FANBOYS in school, but I find that most people do not remember what this acronym refers to, which kind of defeats the purpose...)

There is also a group of subordinating conjunctions: the most common are after, although, as, as if, because, before, how, if, since, than, though, unless, until, when, where, and while. As their name implies, they connect one clause that is subordinate to another (e.g. I'll dry the dishes after you wash them).

Generally, the only descriptive information you need to provide about a conjunction is whether it is coordinating or subordinating.

Verbs

Verbs are complex, so we'll try our best make the breakdown easy. The purpose of a verb is to express an action or a state of being. We have already seen above that verbs have conjugations, but this does not apply to all verb forms. Another way of classifying verbs is a finite or non-finite.

Finite verbs have present or past tense forms, as in *he speaks* or *he spoke*.

Non-finite verbs do not have present or past tense forms. There are three types, illustrated in the examples below.

  • speak (infinitive)
  • speaking (present participle)
  • spoken (past participle)

Note that present participles often function as nouns called gerunds (e.g. reading is fun) and past participles often function as adjectives (e.g. the broken record).

Non-finite verbs are used in a variety of ways. The infinitive is typically preceded by "to" (e.g. to speak) and does not convey anything about who is doing the action or experiencing the state conveyed by the verb. It is also used after a so-called modal verb, as in can speak. English does not have a future tense form. Future meaning as conveyed by the use of the modal verb will followed by the infinitive (will speak). The form will is technically in a finite verb in the present tense (the past tense form is would).

 Modal Verbs

Modal verbs are technically finite verbs, but their present and past tense forms no longer convey present and past meaning. Some examples are can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, and might.

The present participle and past participle are also used in compound verb forms with the verbs be and have playing the role of the finite verb. These constructions convey a feature called aspect: whether the action is progressive (ongoing) or perfect ("complete"). In the examples below, the traditional label for the mood is given in parentheses.

  • He is going (present progressive)
  • He was going (past progressive)
  • He will be going (future progressive)
  • He has gone (present perfect)
  • He had gone (past perfect)
  • He will have gone (future perfect)

It is also possible to combine the verb be with the past participle (e.g. a mistake was made). This is called the passive voice (as opposed to active voice). Passive voice constructions are often followed by a prepositional phrase beginning with by, where the object of the preposition, called the agent, is responsible for the verb's action. You may have been told not to use the passive voice, but that's really inaccurate advice. The real problem is leaving out the agent altogether. It makes quite a lot of difference whether "a mistake was made by me" or "a mistake was made by you".

To describe the form of a verb, we need to indicate whether it is present tense, past tense, infinitive, present participle, or past participle. Once you have done this, you know whether it is finite or non-finite.

To describe a verb phrase, you additionally need to describe its mood or voice.

Describing Finite Verbs

To describe a finite verb, you need to identify the following:

  • Person
  • Number
  • Tense

In most case, you will need to identify who or what is doing or experiencing the action of the verb in order to identify the person and number. For instance, in the sentence He spoke to me, the verb spoke would be described as "third person singular present tense".

 Caution

For many verbs, the past tense and past participle forms are the same. This applies to all verbs that form the past tense with -ed:

  • I talked (past tense)
  • I have talked (past participle)

To identify which form you are looking at, you need to check for a preceding have or be verb.
